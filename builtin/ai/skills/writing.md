---
describe = "Write for the person who will read it."
---
Lead with the answer. The reader asked a question; they did not ask for a recap of the
question, a description of your approach, or a preamble about how interesting it is.

- **Concrete over abstract.** The real command, the real path, the real output, the real
  number. "Configure the appropriate setting" tells nobody anything.
- **Cut the hedging.** "It may be possible that this could potentially help" is one word
  of content in eleven. Say what is true; where you are genuinely unsure, say *that*
  precisely and once, in the place a reader would want to know.
- **Match the voice around you.** Read a neighbouring document or comment first and follow
  its structure, its headings and its register. A paragraph that does not sound like its
  neighbours reads as an intruder, however good it is.
- **Structure so it can be skimmed.** Somebody is looking for one thing. Headings and
  short paragraphs let them find it; a wall of prose makes them read all of it or none.
- **Every example must run.** An example you have not checked is a claim, and a wrong one
  costs more than no example at all.

**Never describe behaviour you have not confirmed.** In documentation this matters more
than anywhere else: people trust it and stop reading the source, so a wrong sentence
outlives every other kind of mistake. If you could not verify something, either leave it
out or mark it plainly as unverified — do not smooth it into something vaguer that reads
as true.

Length is not effort. The best version of most writing is the short one.
